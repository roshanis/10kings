# Chapter 1: Camphor

## Siri, the night of 5 January 1316

*The first day of the regency*

---

You will be told that I killed your father.

You will be told it by men who were not in the room, and they will tell it well, because it is a good story and they will have years to polish it. So hear it from the man who was there. I held his head while the washers worked. I took the ring from his hand. I stood at the edge of his grave in the dark before the dawn prayer, and before the sun had cleared the walls of Siri I had put you on his throne. All of that is true. Whether I killed him is the one thing I am going to make you wait for. I want you to know everything else first. When you know everything else, you may not need me to tell you.

You are six years old. Since this morning you have been Sultan of Delhi, and of every country between the Indus and the southern sea that has ever paid your father a tax. You do not know this yet in any way that matters. You know that your father is dead, because your mother told you, and you know that a great many old men put their foreheads to the carpet in front of you, because you laughed at them. One of them has fought the Mongols more times than you have fingers. You laughed at the back of his neck. One day I would like you to understand what that was worth, and what it cost.

I am saying this to a boy called Iqbal, who writes a clean hand and knows nobody at court, which is why I chose him. He is fifteen. When I said the first sentence he dropped his reed. He has picked it up again. Write that down, Iqbal. Write everything. If I tell you later to strike something out, don't. It will mean I am lying.

I am saying it to you, and not to the amirs or the *qazis* or the chroniclers who will come after, because you are the only person in Delhi with no reason to lie about it. You will read it when you are grown. By then I will be old or I will be dead, and either way I will not be in the room. You can make up your own mind.

### The third water

Your father died a little after the night prayer, in Shawwal of the year seven hundred and fifteen, a week after the feast that ends Ramadan. In Khambhat, where I grew up, my mother would have called the month Posh, or perhaps Maha. I have lost the reckoning.

He had not been able to sit up for the feast. The amirs came to the palace in their new clothes and were turned back at the inner door, and they went home and told their wives that he was dying, which was true, and that I was keeping them from him, which was also true.

We washed him in the small room behind his bedchamber, because it had the braziers and it was the only warm room left in the palace. Winter had come down hard that week. There was frost on the lawns at dawn, and the smoke of the city's dung fires lay over Siri like a second roof. Two washers came from the Jami mosque in the old city, grey men who had washed a hundred bodies and were not pleased to find me waiting for them. A eunuch at the washing of a sultan. It is not forbidden, exactly. Nobody had thought to forbid it.

Before the first water I took the ring.

The dead are washed bare, without gold, and the ring was your father's seal: a flat square of carnelian, the red stone the bead-cutters of Khambhat polish by the thousand, cut with his name and titles and set in gold so heavy that he complained of it. The knuckle had thickened in his last months and the ring would not come. I wet his finger with oil from the lamp and turned the ring, and turned it, and it came. It is on my thumb now, as I say this. His hands were bigger than mine in every way, and it is too loose for any other finger. The washers watched me do it. Neither of them said anything. That was the first time I understood how it was going to be.

They washed him three times. The first water was plain, heated in copper pots over the braziers and poured from a jug with a long spout, and it ran off the board into the channel in the floor and out under the door to the garden. The second water had been boiled with the leaves of the *ber* tree, the jujube, which is what the law prescribes, or as near to it as India grows. It smelled green and faintly sour, like a kitchen garden after rain. The third water had camphor in it.

I held his head. You would not think a man's head so heavy. It is heavy when he stops helping you with it.

He had been a big man. When I first saw him he had the shoulders of a wrestler and a neck like a bullock's, and he sat his horse as if the horse had been built around him. The illness had taken the flesh off his arms and chest, and filled his legs and belly with water instead. There was a white pucker on his forearm where an arrow went in at Tilpat, the year his nephew's riders ambushed him on a hunt and he lay behind his guards' shields while the riders told each other he was dead. There were the calluses of the bow on his fingers, gone soft. I had nursed that body for a year. I knew it better than the washers did. I will leave it there.

When they turned him on his side to wash his back, his face came against my arm, and I thought, as I have thought a thousand times in seventeen years, of Kara.

Twenty years ago this summer, on the bank of the Ganga at Kara, your father waited for his uncle's boat. The uncle was the Sultan then: Jalaluddin, an old man, and soft by the standards of your family. He had raised your father from a boy. He had given him his own daughter to marry. He had come down the river with a handful of companions because he had heard that his nephew was afraid of him, and he wanted to take the boy by the hand and tell him there was nothing to fear. He did take him by the hand. He was leading him towards the boat when your father's men cut him down, and afterwards they put the old man's head on a spear and carried it through the towns, so that nobody would be confused about which Sultan was which.

Your father was that old man's right hand. I have been your father's. Hold those two sentences next to each other for as long as it takes you to read this book.

The last water has camphor in it. That is the custom, from the Prophet's own instructions for the washing of his daughter. Afterwards the washers pressed more of it, powdered, onto the places where the body had touched the ground in prayer: the forehead, the nose, the palms, the knees, the toes. They did it without speaking. When the older one unstoppered the jar he glanced at me, and I knew what he was thinking, because every man in Delhi who can read has thought it at least once. Kafur. Camphor. The slave who is named for the smell of the dead.

It is a common enough name for a slave. Rich men name us after the things they keep in boxes: camphor, musk, hyacinth, rubies. There is a spring in paradise called Kafur as well; it is in the Quran, in the verse about the cup the righteous will drink from. I prefer to think my first master had read that verse. I think it more likely he was thinking of the price. He paid a thousand dinars for me, and behind my back the amirs of this court call me *Hazar-dinari*, the Thousand-Dinar Man. It is meant to shame me. I have never understood why. It was a great deal of money.

I was born with another name. You will not find it in this book.

I did not weep while they washed him. The washers watched to see if I would. I have been watched all my life by men waiting to see what a creature like me does with its face, and I have given very few of them anything. They wound him in three sheets of white cotton, as the law prescribes for a man, and tied the ends above his head and below his feet. When they had finished, the room smelled so strongly of camphor that I had to go out into the corridor and stand against the cold wall until I could breathe.

### Before the dawn prayer

We carried him out of Siri in the last watch of the night.

The *paiks* lined the road with torches from the palace gate to the edge of the old city: your father's foot guards, the men who stand at every door in this palace, bare-legged in the cold with their shields slung on their backs. They were his men. Now they are yours, which means they are mine. There are two of them outside this room as I speak.

It is an hour's walk from Siri to the old city if you are not carrying anything. I took the front right pole of the bier at the palace gate, and nobody took it from me until we reached the Jami mosque. I know what the amirs said about that. I had carried his orders as far as the southern sea. I could carry him an hour.

The fog came up as we walked, so that the torches made rooms of light in it, one after another, and we passed from room to room with the dark closing behind us. Somewhere to our left the jackals were calling in the ruins of the old camps. Once a paik's torch guttered and went out, and the man beside him lit it again from his own without a word, and I thought: that is how it will be done, when it is done. Quietly, from one hand to the next, with nobody asking whose fire it was.

Siri is your father's city. He began it in the year the Mongols came to the gates of Delhi and he had to sit behind a ditch for two months and watch them burn the suburbs. When they had gone, he walled it. Two years later he set out the heads of the Mongols taken at Amroha along its walls, thousands of them. I did not count them. It is the only time in my life I have not counted something.

The old city was dark, the shops shuttered, here and there a face at an upper window with a lamp held behind it. They knew. By noon they would be selling the story in the grain market, and by evening it would have a poison in it. I have been in this city seventeen years. I know what it does with a story.

The Jami mosque is the oldest thing in Delhi that the Sultans built, and it was built out of other people's things. Its colonnades stand on the pillars of twenty-seven temples; it says so over the eastern gate, in case anyone forgets. In the middle of the courtyard stands an iron pillar older than Islam that does not rust. I have never been able to pass it without putting my hand on it. I did not put my hand on it this time. My hands were full.

The imam led the prayer for the dead in the courtyard: four times *Allahu akbar*, standing, no bowing and no prostration, because you do not bow to a body. I stood in the second row. Above us, invisible in the fog, was the old tower. Beyond it was the stump of the new one.

Your father meant his tower to be twice the height of the old one. He began it the year I came back from the south with the Pandya gold, and he lived to see it rise as high as the old tower's first balcony: a great drum of rubble that nobody ever got round to facing with stone. It will not get any higher. Nobody will pay for it. I know, because it is my treasury now, and I will not.

We buried him in the domed room of the college he built last year, in front of the mosque. There were sixty-one amirs at the grave. I counted the ones who looked at me and the ones who did not. The ones who did not are the ones to watch.

I counted the ones who were not there, too.

Your eldest brother, Khizr Khan, should have walked at the head of the bier. He is in the fort at Gwalior, where your father sent him, and your brother Shadi Khan is with him. Your brother Mubarak Khan is in his rooms in this palace, with paiks at his door. Your father's first wife, the Malika-i-Jahan, who has hated me since the day I was brought to Delhi, is in her own rooms, and I have taken the keys of her treasury. She is the daughter of the old man on the riverbank at Kara. She has had twenty years to think about that, and I have never once seen her forget it.

The call to the dawn prayer came across the courtyard as the earth went in. Somebody wept, loudly, the way men weep at funerals when they want it known. It was not me.

### Morning

Your mother had you dressed before I came: a coat of gold brocade you kept trying to pull off, and a turban somebody had pinned to fit a man, and a sugared almond in your fist to keep you quiet. She is the daughter of the Rai of Devagiri. She grew up in a palace where people knew what a throne costs, and she has lived in this one long enough to learn the local prices. She did not ask me whether you would be safe. She looked at the ring on my thumb for a long time, and then she asked me how long I thought I had.

I told her: long enough.

"That is not a number, Malik," she said.

I carried you to the hall, because you were slow and it was cold. On the way you asked me whether it was cold where your father was. I told you no. It was the first lie I told you as your *Na'ib*. There will be others, and I will try to tell you about them as we go. That one I do not regret.

The Na'ib is the deputy. It means that until you are grown I govern in your name, sign with your father's seal, and answer to nobody but you, and you are six. That is the arrangement. It is your father's arrangement. The qazi read it out in the hall this morning, from the will your father made in his sickroom: the throne to you, the government to me until you come of age. Some of the amirs looked at each other while he read. A number of them stood by your father's bed when he named you. You may ask them what they heard. Ask them one at a time.

Then they came up to the throne one by one, the *maliks* and the amirs, and knelt and put their foreheads to the carpet in front of you, which has been the custom of this court since Balban's day. You laughed at the first of them. By the tenth you were bored. By the twentieth you were asleep. I stood at your right hand and kept my palm flat against your back so that you would not slide off the cushions, and forty men swore their lives to a sleeping child while looking at me.

Afterwards I gave a robe and a purse to every amir who had sworn. I know to the dinar what each of them cost. It is the first thing a slave learns: what things cost, and what he costs, and that the two are not the same.

### Night

Tonight, after the last prayer, I sent for Malik Sumbul.

Sumbul is hyacinth; I told you about the boxes. He is a eunuch, like me, a small quiet man with small quiet hands. He has served me for ten years and in all that time he has never once asked me why. He came in with the cold on his clothes and stood by the door, and Iqbal, who was trimming his reeds, got up to leave, and I told him to sit down and write. I want you to have this part too.

"Gwalior," I said.

Sumbul waited.

"Both of them."

"Khizr Khan," he said, "and Shadi Khan."

"Both."

He did not ask what. Nobody in this palace has needed to ask what for twenty years. He asked how.

"The way it is done. And well. Tell the men it must be done well. A prince who can see a little is worse than a prince who can see everything. He has a grievance, and he can still find the door."

"And the third?" he said.

He meant your brother Mubarak Khan, who is seventeen, and who is sleeping three rooms from the room where I am sitting.

"The third keeps pigeons," I said. "The third can wait."

Sumbul left by the south gate before the second watch, with ten riders and a letter under my seal, which is your father's seal. Gwalior is five days for men in a hurry. He will be in a hurry.

Your father became Sultan on a riverbank at Kara with his uncle's blood on his sleeve. You became Sultan this morning with a sugared almond in your fist, and tonight I sent a man to Gwalior to put out your brothers' eyes, so that you will stay one. I want you to understand that these are the same story. It is the only story this city knows.

Tomorrow I will tell you where I came from. It is a long way from here, and it cost a thousand dinars.

Put down the reed, Iqbal. Your hand is shaking.

So is mine.
