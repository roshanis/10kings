# The Right Hand

*A novel of Malik Kafur, the slave who ruled Delhi for thirty-five days.* The title and subtitle are working names.

This is a new project, separate from *Blood and Thrones*. It draws on that book's research on Alauddin Khilji, but it has its own narrator and its own form.

## The story

Delhi, January 1316. Alauddin Khilji is dead: the Sultan who beat the Mongols, fixed the price of grain, and became Sultan by having his uncle killed on a riverbank. The man at his bedside holds the seal.

Malik Kafur was bought as a boy for a thousand dinars, then taken as loot from a merchant's house in Khambhat. He became the greatest general of his age. He carried Delhi's armies to Warangal and Madurai, and he came back with elephants by the hundred.

Now Kafur has put a six-year-old on the throne and sent a man to Gwalior to blind the heirs. He has thirty-five days to live. He spends them telling the truth to the boy.

## Why this story

- **It is true.** In the chronicles, Kafur went from slave to general to regent, and was murdered 35 days after his master died, by the palace guards who had served that master.
- **It has a shape the reader can feel coming.** In this dynasty, the right hand kills the head, again and again:
  - In 1296 Alauddin, his uncle's right hand, has the uncle killed at Kara.
  - In 1316 Kafur, Alauddin's right hand, is accused of hastening his death.
  - In 1316 Alauddin's son Mubarak has Kafur killed.
  - In 1320 Mubarak's favourite, Khusrau Khan, kills Mubarak.
  - In 1320 Ghazi Malik Tughluq, who once rode in Kafur's vanguard, kills Khusrau Khan and ends the line.
- **Nobody has heard this narrator.**
  - He is a slave, a eunuch, and a convert from Gujarat.
  - He is the most capable man in Delhi and its most despised.
  - His readers may know him from the film *Padmaavat* (2018). The history is stranger than the film.
- **There is a clock.** The reader knows from the cover how the 35 days end. Kafur half-knows.
- **There is an open question at the centre.** Barani, writing about forty years later, repeats the charge that Kafur killed the Sultan. The book lets the reader decide.

## The frame

Kafur dictates the book at night during his regency, to a fifteen-year-old scribe called Iqbal. He addresses it to the boy-sultan, Shihabuddin Umar, to read when he is grown.

- **Now:** each night's dictation begins with what Kafur did that day as regent.
- **Then:** Kafur tells his life in order, from Khambhat in 1299 to the Sultan's deathbed.
- **The clocks meet:**
  - The last Then chapter is the night Alauddin died.
  - The last Now chapter is the night the paiks come.
  - A one-page coda in Iqbal's hand tells what happened at the door.
- **The Historical Note** says what became of the boy. Mubarak blinded him in the spring of 1316 and had him killed at Gwalior in 1318, so he never read a word of the book written for him.

See `outline.md` for the chapter plan.

## A novel, not narrative history

*Blood and Thrones* is narrative history told through invented witnesses. This book is a historical novel narrated by a real man, in the tradition of Robert Graves's Claudius, Marguerite Yourcenar's Hadrian and Hilary Mantel's Cromwell.

`research_original_kings_review.md` faults chapters that use real people as invented narrators. The fault there was invention passed off as history, and dates that contradicted the record. Here the fiction is declared, and it keeps three rules:

1. **Never contradict the record where it speaks.**
2. **Invent freely where the record is silent, and say so in the Historical Note.**
3. **Where the sources disagree, let Kafur take a side, and flag it.** For example, Khusrau counts 512 elephants from the south and Barani 612. Kafur counted them himself.

## Kafur's voice

- **He counts everything:**
  - the amirs at a grave, and which of them looked at him;
  - what each oath cost;
  - elephants.
  - The one thing he did not count was the Mongol heads at Siri.
- **He is plain, dry and exact.** Short sentences. He mocks the ornament of Persian court prose; this book is the opposite of a chronicle.
- **He explains the court to a child.** That lets the reader in without a lecture.
- **Feeling shows only in the body.** He leaves a room, holds a head, or wears a ring on his thumb because it fits no other finger.
- **He lies to the boy sometimes, and says so.**
- **He never gives his birth name.** It is the only thing he owns that nobody paid for.
- **Avoid:**
  - modern idiom, and hours and minutes (use watches);
  - em-dashes (see AGENTS.md).

## Shape and length

- About 100,000 words: 24 chapters of about 4,000 words, plus a coda and a Historical Note.
- Three parts:
  - **The Thousand Dinars:** 1299 to 1303.
  - **The South:** 1305 to 1311.
  - **The Sickroom:** 1313 to 1316.

## The hard material

- **Violence.** The record is full of it: blindings, massacres, captives trampled by elephants, families punished for their men. Show the consequence, not the spectacle. Kafur's flat register makes it worse, and that is the point.
- **The temples.** The southern raids stripped temples, and Amir Khusrau celebrates it. Show it through the record and through Kafur's divided eyes, a convert born Hindu. Do not gloat, and do not sanitise.
- **Kafur's body.** Tell the castration once, briefly, in chapter 2. After that, the court's contempt and his own view of himself carry it.
- **Kafur and the Sultan.** Barani says the Sultan was infatuated with him. The novel leaves the nature of their love to the reader, as chapter 1 does: "I had nursed that body for a year. I knew it better than the washers did. I will leave it there."

## What is recorded and what is invented

**Recorded:**
- The capture at Khambhat in 1299 and the thousand dinars.
- The Ravi command in 1306.
- The southern campaigns of 1308, 1310, 1311 and 1313.
- The recall in 1315, Alp Khan's execution, and Khizr Khan's imprisonment.
- Alauddin's death on 4 January 1316 and his burial at the Qutb.
- The six-year-old's enthronement.
- Malik Sumbul's errand to Gwalior, and the blinding of Khizr Khan and Shadi Khan.
- The Malika-i-Jahan's imprisonment.
- The attempt on Mubarak, Kafur's killing by the paiks about 35 days after the Sultan's death, and his unmarked grave.

**Reported by one late source:**
- Kafur's marriage to Jhatyapali (Firishta, about 1607).

**Invented:**
- Kafur's birth name, family, birthplace and age.
- The scribe Iqbal and the dictation.
- Every conversation.
- The seal ring's carnelian.
- The Sultan's symptoms.
- Mubarak's pigeons.
- Kafur's presence at events where the record does not place him.

## Sources

The facts in these notes were checked against modern summaries of the sources below, not against the sources themselves. Check each against the originals before drafting the chapter that uses it.

**Primary, in translation:**
- Ziauddin Barani, *Tarikh-i Firuz Shahi* (about 1357). Excerpts are in H. M. Elliot and John Dowson, *The History of India as Told by Its Own Historians*, vol. 3 (1871).
- Amir Khusrau, *Khaza'in ul-Futuh* (1311), an eyewitness account of the campaigns, translated by Muhammad Habib as *The Campaigns of 'Ala'u'd-Din Khilji* (1931).

- Isami, *Futuh us-Salatin* (1350), translated by A. Mahdi Husain.
- Firishta, *Tarikh-i Firishta* (about 1607), translated by John Briggs (1829).
- Marco Polo, on Cambaet (Khambhat), in the 1290s.

**Modern:**

## Decisions for you

1. **Title.** *The Right Hand*, *The Thousand-Dinar Man*, or *Camphor*.
2. **The frame.** The recommendation is Kafur dictating to the boy, as drafted. The alternatives are:
   - Kafur speaking to the dead Sultan;
   - close third person.
3. **The central question.** Does the book say whether Kafur killed Alauddin? The recommendation is that chapter 23 tells what Kafur saw and did, and still leaves room for doubt.
4. **Length and parts.**
5. **The link to *Blood and Thrones*.** Its Alauddin chapter is due for a rewrite. The two books should agree on every fact.
6. **Where the project lives.** It could stay in this folder or move to its own repository.
