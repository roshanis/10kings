# The Original Ten: Restart Review

*Working notes, not for publication. Written on 27 September 2026, when the manuscript returned to its original ten kings.*

This review asks one question of each chapter: what should survive the restart?

**How it was done:**
- Each chapter was read in full against AGENTS.md and checked against the chronicles, inscriptions and scholarship.
- The most consequential claims were then verified a second time.
- Web pages could only be read through search summaries, so a few leads are marked "verify" before use.

## The Verdict

The restart is warranted. Nine of the ten chapters need rewriting, and one needs substantial revision.

The trouble is not polish. In most chapters, the headline claim, the narrator or the central set-piece fails against the sources.

The good news is just as consistent. In every chapter the attested story is at least as dramatic as the invented one, and every chapter has scenes or lines worth carrying into the rewrite.

## Decisions Before Writing

1. **Title.** Two are in play: *The Forgotten Kings: Ten Rulers Who Shaped the Destiny of India*, and *Blood and Thrones: The Unknown Kings Who Forged India*.
   - The problems are in the subtitles:
     - Lachit was not a king.
     - Alauddin is not unknown in India.
   - Settle the title before any cover or KDP work.
2. **Order.** Reign order with a Colachel prologue, or the current order. The case for reign order is below.
3. **Lineup.** This review assumes all ten stay. The pressure points:
   - two Delhi sultans;
   - no king between 1055 and 1210 or between 1467 and 1671;
   - no Rajasthan, Bengal, Kashmir, Gujarat or Punjab.
4. **Length.** The chapters now run from 2,583 to 9,783 words.
   - Aim for 5,000 to 6,500 words each, 50,000 to 65,000 in all.
   - That fits the KDP plan of 45,000+ words and 180 to 220 pages.

## Problems That Run Through the Book

These show up in most chapters, so fixing them is one job, not ten.

1. **The notes misstate what is real.** The Editor's Notes and Historical Notes are where a reader learns what to trust, and they are the least reliable part of the manuscript.
   - **Real people labelled fictional:**
     - Imad-ud-din Raihan (chapter 7) was Balban's real rival, killed around 1255.
     - Abu Nasr al-Utbi (chapter 10) was Mahmud of Ghazni's real secretary, dead by about 1040.
     - Chapter 3's "Captain João Ribeiro" shares the name and rank of a real Portuguese officer and author of the period.
   - **Attested history labelled invented:**
     - Bhoja's three-king alliance (recorded in Chalukya inscriptions).
     - "General Abachi" in chapter 2 (he is in Barani).
   - **Invention labelled attested:**
     - The Pandya tribute terms (chapter 5).
     - "Later traditions" about Vidyadhara's warfare (chapter 9).
     - "Authenticated Islamic military accounts" of Kapilendra (chapter 8).
   - **Scenes that were cut from the text:**
     - Chapter 5's alliance with Balban.
     - Chapter 10's letters and metallurgy manuscript.
   - **The Author's Note repeats the problems.** `Author_Historical_Note.md` repeats most of these errors.
   - **The fix:** write every note last, against the final text, and put each note through the fact-check.
2. **Headlines the sources don't support.** Every chapter's headline claim overclaims or contradicts the record. That claim may sit in the title, subtitle, KDP blurb line or Historical Note:
   - the "first decisive defeat" of a European power (1);
   - grain prices "stopped" the Mongols (2);
   - the Ahoms "unconquered" (3);
   - captivity as the forge of the empire (4);
   - the "Pandya lake" (5);
   - Marathi made official (6);
   - *dagh* and *chehra* credited to Balban (7);
   - "largest since the Mauryas" (8);
   - Mahmud "forced to retreat" (9);
   - Bhoja "rewrote the rules of power" (10).

   The KDP package needs rewriting alongside the chapters.
3. **Dates disagree.** Subtitles and notes disagree with each other, or with the record, in chapters 2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10. The chapter sections below give the corrected reigns.
4. **Anachronisms of word and weapon.**
   - **Identity words:** "Rajput" in the 11th century.
   - **Weapons:** the katar before Vijayanagara; the talwar before the Sultanate.
   - **Place and group names:** "Moplah", "Bundelkhand", "Bhojshala".
   - **Ranks:** "Captain" and "Sergeant" in Indian and Ghaznavid armies.
   - **Modern concepts:** "medieval" in a medieval narrator's mouth; Adam Smith's "invisible hand"; Bismarck's "blood and iron".
   - **Overused words:** the same few words recur across chapters: "system", "innovation", "coordination", "useful".
5. **One narrator, ten names.** Most narrators sound like the same modern essayist, and several chapters end with two or three sections of commentary instead of scene. The template repeats:
   - **Chapters 2 and 7** are both told from a Delhi death cell, with the same chain sores and bones breaking like "green wood".
   - **Chapters 4 and 8** both kill a companion named Yusuf in the first battle, leave his blood on the narrator, and give blood the taste of "copper and salt".
   - **Chapters 1, 2, 3, 4 and 7** end on the same beat: the narrator realises he was only the king's instrument.
   - **Chapters 6 to 10** close on a sweeping abstraction instead of an image. Chapter 10's last words are "the future of human civilization itself".
   - **The fix:** each witness needs a distinct voice, a physical position in events, and a stake.
6. **Faith as the plot.** Several chapters turn their king's wars into Hindu resistance against Islam:
   - "witness to the limits of Islamic expansion" (chapter 9)
   - "the unprecedented challenge posed by Hindu resurgence" (chapter 8)
   - a plan to "eliminate Islamic power" (chapter 8)

   The sources show alliances and armies that crossed faith lines:
   - Bukka styled himself "Sultan among Hindu kings".
   - Kapilendra's longest wars were against Hindu kings, and his son rode with a Bahmani army.
   - Vidyadhara's feudatory killed the king of Kannauj for submitting to Mahmud, and Vidyadhara then made his own bargain with him.

   Every conflict can stay; the framing should follow the sources. That nuance is also part of the Dalrymple standard in AGENTS.md.
7. **Two live sensitivities.** For both, state what the sources say, and say where they stop.
   - **Bhojshala (chapter 10):** the site is in active litigation.
   - **Chittor and Padmini (chapter 2):** the jauhar legend is contested, and the subject has been inflamed since the film *Padmaavat*.

### The lives collide, and the book doesn't use it

Read in reign order, these stories keep crashing into each other:

- **Vidyadhara and Bhoja:**
  - They were contemporaries and neighbours.
  - A fragmentary Chandela inscription from Mahoba claims that Bhoja and "Kalachuri-Chandra" (Gangeyadeva) worshipped Vidyadhara "like pupils". Some historians read it as an invasion Vidyadhara repulsed; others, such as K. M. Munshi, as boast.
  - Neither chapter mentions the other king.
- **Simhana II's Devagiri:** this is where Alauddin began. His 1296 raid on Simhana's great-grandson Ramachandra paid for the murder of his uncle and his seizure of the throne.
- **Jatavarman's Pandya kingdom:** Malik Kafur sacked it in 1311, in the middle of a Pandya succession war.
- **Bukka's Vijayanagara:** it rose after the Tughluq sack of Kampili in 1327, which is where chapter 4 opens.
- **Kapilendra:** he fought the Bahmani Sultanate and Vijayanagara, the two states born from the Tughluq collapse in the Deccan.
- **Lachit and Marthanda Varma:**
  - Ram Singh was sent to Assam as punishment for letting Shivaji escape his custody at Agra.
  - That gives a bridge to a Maratha narrator in Travancore seventy years later.

In reign order, the book becomes one story: how India was forged by these collisions over 750 years. It ends with Lachit and Marthanda Varma facing the Mughal and European powers.

**Recommendation:**
- Use reign order, with a short prologue at Colachel: the shot that fired the Dutch powder store on 5 August 1741.
- The book then opens at full voltage and pays off in its final chapter.
- The cost is renumbering every file, so this is the author's call.

### Gaps

- **1055 to 1210:** 155 years with no king, including the Ghurid conquest of 1192.
- **1467 to 1671:** two centuries with no king, including:
  - the Mughal conquest;
  - Vijayanagara's peak and its fall at Talikota (1565);
  - the Deccan sultanates.
- **Geography:**
  - two central Indian kings;
  - two Delhi sultans;
  - one Deccan king;
  - three in the far south;
  - one each in Odisha and Assam;
  - none from Rajasthan, Gujarat, Punjab, Kashmir, Bengal or Sindh.

The December 2025 swaps (Rana Kumbha, Alauddin Husain Shah, Zain-ul-Abidin, Chand Bibi, and Dahir then Suhaldev) filled exactly these gaps. If that balance still matters, one chapter from `alternates/` could replace one original rather than abandoning the lineup. Of the alternates, Suhaldev has the weakest evidence: he first appears in the *Mirat-i-Masudi*, written about six centuries after the battle it describes.

On faith, eight of the ten are Hindu. The two Muslim rulers are both Delhi sultans, and both are drawn as architects of fear. Chapter 4's premise, a Mappila Muslim in a Hindu king's service, points the right way, though its plot then turns on a purity purge the record does not support. See "Faith as the plot" above.

### The promise on the cover

- **"Unknown":** all ten are unknown to a general English-language reader. Several are famous in India, so the book has to decide which reader "unknown" means:
  - Alauddin Khalji is among the best-known sultans, and a screen villain since *Padmaavat* (2018).
  - Lachit is now a national figure; the Indian government marked his 400th birth anniversary in 2022.
  - Raja Bhoja is proverbial.
- **"Kings":**
  - Lachit was the Ahom commander, the *Borphukan*, not the king.
  - A subtitle that says "rulers" covers him; "kings" does not.
  - Or the chapter puts his king on stage (see chapter 3).
- **Title:** two are in play:
  - *The Forgotten Kings: Ten Rulers Who Shaped the Destiny of India* (manuscript, cover spec, visual specs);
  - *Blood and Thrones: The Unknown Kings Who Forged India* (AGENTS.md, KDP package).

### The very first plan

Before any chapter was committed, the first concept had three kings in place of Kapilendra, Vidyadhara and Bhoja Paramara. That concept is the KDP draft and research plan in the initial commit. The three kings were:
- **Raja Dahir** of Sindh (d. 712)
- **Mihira Bhoja Pratihara** (r. 836-885)
- **Govinda III Rashtrakuta** (r. 793-814)

Only Dahir was ever drafted, and only briefly. If the restart wants to reach back before 1000 CE, Mihira Bhoja and Govinda III are strong and genuinely little-known candidates.

## Chapter by Chapter

Each section can serve as the brief for that chapter's rewrite.

### 1. Marthanda Varma: REWRITE

*Travancore, r. 1729-1758. 5,429 words. Narrator: Bhimrao Holkar, a fictional Maratha cavalry officer hired to drill horse.*

**Where it stands**
- The prose is the most polished in the book, and the envy of De Lannoy is a real emotional thread.
- But the chapter covers only 1741-42, and its battle is not the one that was fought.
- Its first page stakes the book's credibility on its most falsifiable claim.

**What's wrong**
- **The headline claim is false.** The Historical Note says Colachel was "the first decisive defeat of a European colonial power by an Indian kingdom on Indian soil". Earlier defeats include:
  - the Mughals taking Portuguese Hooghly in 1632;
  - the English Company's envoys prostrating themselves before Aurangzeb and paying an indemnity to end the Child's War (1686-90);
  - the Zamorin razing the Portuguese fort at Chaliyam, on the Malabar coast itself, in 1571;
  - the Marathas taking Vasai from the Portuguese in May 1739, two years before Colachel.

  Vasai also breaks the chapter from inside, because its narrator is a Maratha veteran of the Konkan wars. The research notes' "first Asian" version fails too: Koxinga expelled the Dutch from Taiwan in 1662.
- **The battle isn't Colachel.**
  - The decisive act is an invented cavalry charge, but Kerala armies fought on foot.
  - Colachel was the siege of a Dutch stockade. On 5 August 1741 a Travancore shot fired its powder store, and the garrison capitulated days later; 10 August is the commemorated date.
- **The Arcot horse is implausible as written.**
  - In March 1740 Chanda Sahib's Arcot army invaded Travancore, beat Ramayyan Dalawa, and was bought off with 120,000 gold coins and six elephants.
  - By March 1741 the Marathas had taken Trichinopoly and captured Chanda Sahib.
  - A regiment formally hired from the Nawab makes no sense. Freelance riders from Arcot's broken army would, and they would make a better story.
- **De Lannoy:** he was not the Dutch commander. He rose to *Valiya Kappithan* ("great captain") over years.
- **Travancore's politics:**
  - The Ettuveetil Pillamar were crushed in the early 1730s. A Kazhakoottam lord, from one of the eight houses, riding beside the narrator in 1741 is impossible.
  - Brahmin conspirators were exiled, not executed.
  - The threat to Marthanda Varma came from his uncle's sons, the Thampis, not from his uncle.
- **The treaty scene is invented.** Peace came at Mavelikkara on 15 August 1753, with the Dutch agreeing to sell arms to their conqueror.
- **Overreach:**
  - "Crushed Dutch power" goes too far: the Dutch kept Cochin until 1795.
  - "Tiger of Travancore" is not a period epithet, and it echoes Tipu Sultan, who later attacked Travancore.
- **Undisclosed invention:**
  - the charge;
  - the palace burnings;
  - a king who secretly speaks Dutch;
  - the aunt's poisoned sweetmeat, an invented anecdote in a real king's mouth.
- **The narrator's name:** "Holkar" reads as kin to Malhar Rao Holkar, who commanded at Vasai, whatever the note says.
- **AGENTS.md:** several of its sample sentences were lifted from this chapter:
  - the opening line;
  - the sweetmeat;
  - "iron-shod hooves... Tamil steel";
  - "builds schools".

  When the chapter is rewritten, refresh the samples so the new chapters don't echo the old one.

**What works**
- "De Lannoy's smile thinned. 'You handle his horses.'"
- "a cold decision that I was more useful honed than discarded"
- "where the monsoon smells of pepper instead of blood"
- The king's offer of land.

**The restart**
- **The crisis:** stage the real two-front crisis of 1740-41, with Arcot by land and the Dutch Company by sea: siege, shot, surrender.
- **The spine:** De Lannoy's slow rise.
- **The ending:** the Thrippadidanam of 1750, when the king laid his sword before Sree Padmanabha and gave his kingdom to the god.
- **The narrator:** rename him. He could be a Vasai veteran who reaches Travancore by way of the Carnatic's wars, unsurprised that Europeans can lose but astonished that the king hires the loser.
- **Framing:** "the king who beat the Dutch East India Company, made a captured officer his general, got the Company to sell him guns, then gave his kingdom to a god."

**Material to use**
- The Thrippadidanam (3 January 1750).
- The Mavelikkara terms.
- Mark de Lannoy's study of the Dutch archives (below).
- De Lannoy's tomb and foundry at Udayagiri.
- Devasahayam Pillai, converted through De Lannoy, executed in 1752 and canonised in 2022.
- The local Mukkuvar fishermen's refusal to help the Dutch.

### 2. Alauddin Khilji: REWRITE, KEEP THE FRAME

*Delhi Sultanate, r. 1296-1316. 9,783 words, the longest chapter. Narrator: Qutlugh Bahadur, a fictional Mongol convert and captain of horse archers, testifying on the night Delhi's converted Mongols are purged.*

**Where it stands**
- The opening roll call of the condemned ("Temur, son of Barak, age eleven." / "Thud.") is the best first page in the book.
- Its frame rests on a real atrocity most readers have never heard of: Mongols given asylum in 1292 fought Delhi's wars for two decades, then were massacred in a single day.
- Almost everything around the frame needs rebuilding, and the chapter should lose a third of its length.

**What's wrong**
- **The purge is misdated.**
  - The note says 1307-1308. Barani's massacre of 20,000 to 30,000 "New Muslims" was in 1311, after a plot to kill the Sultan while he was out hawking.
  - The chapter even has the narrator riding through the Pandya lands (1311), then saying "Ten years later, I wait for execution."
- **Kili (1299) is told backwards.** Zafar Khan's charge did not shatter the Mongol centre. He attacked without orders, was drawn into an ambush by a feigned retreat, and was killed.
- **The 1303 siege is invented.**
  - There was no sortie. Alauddin dug in at the half-built Siri fort and was besieged for two months until the Mongol commander Targhi withdrew.
  - Amroha (1305) and the Ravi (1306), the victories that ended the threat, are missing.
- **The thesis rests on invention.** About 1,700 words of unsourced material carry the "military genius" argument: dung arrows to panic warhorses, and elephant hunts to train the cavalry. The blurb's actual subject, markets and spies, gets about 650 words.
- **Chittor:**
  - Alauddin's leap into the breach is invented.
  - The chapter omits what Amir Khusrau, an eyewitness, records: a surrender followed by the killing of 30,000 people.
  - The jauhar comes from the later Padmini legend and is contested.
- **"General Abachi" is real** (Barani), not invented as the note implies. His plot is merged with the separate hawking plot.
- **Smaller errors:**
  - Akat Khan died after attacking the Sultan at Tilpat in 1301, not "on suspicion".
  - Alauddin was about 32 in 1298, not 43.
  - He renounced wine.
  - Balban had already destroyed "the Forty".
  - The invaders were Chagatai Mongols, not from "Karakorum".
- **Continuity:**
  - The narrator was arrested six hours ago, and also three weeks ago.
  - His age at meeting the Sultan is given as 19, 24 and 22.
  - There are two first meetings, and three characters named Temur.
- **Anachronisms:**
  - "biology", "ghetto", "Sergeant", "Mamluk dynasty".
  - The narrator quotes Barani, who wrote around 1357.
  - Modern American idiom: "couldn't fight worth a damn".
- **Premise:** Alauddin is the least "unknown" king in the lineup, and the blurb's cause and effect is wrong.
  - The price controls came after 1303.
  - The raids ended mainly because the Chagatai khanate fell into succession wars after Duwa's death.

**What works**
- The roll call.
- "Is it Khutulun or Khotulun? We need the correct form for both the death registry and the bill of sale for his wife."
- "I nocked another arrow and put it through his throat before he could speak it aloud."

**The restart**
- **The anchor:** re-anchor the chapter on 1311.
- **The spine:** the converts' real history:
  - the Jalore mutiny and the punishment of the mutineers' families (1299);
  - Mongol deserters fighting for Hammira at Ranthambore;
  - Akat Khan's retainers at Tilpat (1301);
  - the 1305 captives trampled by elephants, their heads displayed at Siri.
- **The battles:** tell Kili and 1303 truthfully.
- **The markets:** make the humiliation at Siri the motive for the price regime, and show that regime through the narrator's own purse.
- **Length:** aim for 6,000 to 6,500 words.
- **Blurb:** "fixed the price of every sack of grain in Delhi to pay the army that beat the Mongols, then massacred the Mongols who had fought for him."

**Material to use**
- Barani on the 1299 punishment and the 1311 massacre.
- The Tilpat attack.
- Barani on Zafar Khan's legend among the Mongols: when their horses would not drink, they asked them, "Did you see Zafar Khan?"
- Barani's market ordinances, and the market superintendent Malik Qabul.
- Khusrau's *Khaza'in ul-Futuh*.
- Siri, and the Alai Darwaza, built in 1311, the year of the massacre.

### 3. Lachit Borphukan: REWRITE

*Ahom Assam; Saraighat, March 1671. 7,637 words. Narrator: "Captain João Ribeiro", a Portuguese gunner in the Mughal army, secretly paid by Lachit.*

**Where it stands**
- It has the best first line in the book: "The shot that killed Admiral Munnawar Khan came from a boat I had told Lachit Borphukan to place there three weeks before the battle began."
- Its voice is cynical and distinct.
- But the premise hands Saraighat to a European mole, which inverts the book's promise to put Indian rulers at the centre of their own stories.
- Lachit is missing from the one moment of his battle that the sources record best.

**What's wrong**
- **The narrator's name belongs to a real man.**
  - Captain João Ribeiro (1622-1693) served the Portuguese in Ceylon until 1658, then went home.
  - He wrote a celebrated history of the island, the *Fatalidade Histórica da Ilha de Ceilão*.
  - The chapter turns a real officer's name and rank into a traitor. Rename him.
- **Lachit is absent at his own turning point.** The chronicles have the ill Lachit carried aboard, advancing with a handful of boats and rallying a fleeing line. The chapter replaces that with an invented sniper plan.
- **Munnawar Khan's death:** he was shot while smoking a hookah. The drawn-sword pose and "sixty years of naval combat" are invented.
- **"Unconquered" is false**, in both the note and the text.
  - Mir Jumla took the Ahom capital Garhgaon in 1662.
  - The Treaty of Ghilajharighat (1663) exacted tribute, territory, and the king's daughter for the Mughal harem.
  - Defensible instead: the Ahom kingdom was never annexed, and Saraighat broke the last great Mughal bid for Guwahati.
- **Lachit was not a king.**
  - He was the *Borphukan*, commander and viceroy of Lower Assam.
  - The chapter names neither Swargadeo (king) of the period, Chakradhwaj Singha or Udayaditya Singha.
- **Dates and places:**
  - The battle was in March 1671, not February.
  - Lachit died at Kaliabor in April 1672, not at Jorhat, which became the capital only in 1794.
  - His burial mound is at Hoolungapara.
  - The 1622 birth year is traditional; write c. 1622.
- **Arms and boats:**
  - The *ramchangi* was a matchlock, not a field piece, and "1,200" comes from Mir Jumla's booty list of 1662.
  - "Poop deck", "mainmast" and "line of battle" are the wrong words for oared river fleets.
- **Undisclosed invention:**
  - Lachit's consumption, and his Portuguese.
  - The Marwari "hawala"; the period instrument was a *hundi*.
  - The note's "mercenary betrayal... well attested", which has no support for this campaign.
- **Anachronisms:** "dead drops", "intelligence picture", "operational timeline", "optimized". "Intelligence" appears 35 times.
- **Craft:**
  - The point of view wobbles between "the Mughal flagship" and "our war galley".
  - The tactics arrive as lecture.
  - The gore exceeds the AGENTS.md limit.
- **Tradition, not record:**
  - The army figures come from Assamese tradition and should be presented that way.
  - The uncle's beheading is also tradition, and the chapter is right to tell it as rumour.

**What works**
- The opening line.
- The Ahom boy "barely sixteen, face serene as a temple carving... Where our swords cut, the dao cleaved".
- "His name appears in no chronicle. Men like us rarely do."
- The handling of the uncle rumour.

**The restart**
- **The outsider:** keep one only as a witness, not the hidden author of victory. A model: Glanius, the Dutch sailor who marched with Mir Jumla in 1662-63 and published his account in 1682.
- **Lachit:** put him on the water at the turning point, in the real geography: the narrows between Itakhuli, Kamakhya and Aswakranta.
- **The plot:** build it on the real intelligence war.
  - Ram Singh's arrow-letter claimed that Lachit had taken a lakh of rupees to abandon Guwahati.
  - The king grew suspicious.
  - The minister Atan Burhagohain defended his general.
  - That test of trust between king and general also answers the "not a king" problem.
- **Opening and ending:**
  - Open on the humiliation of 1662-63.
  - End on the sting: Lachit's brother handed Guwahati back to the Mughals in 1679, and it was retaken only in 1682.
- **Length:** aim for about 5,000 words.

**Material to use**
- Ram Singh's reported admission, in the Assamese chronicles, that he could find no loophole in the Ahom defence.
- Ram Singh's posting to Assam as punishment for Shivaji's escape from his custody. In Sikh tradition, Guru Tegh Bahadur travelled with him.
- The Mughal chronicler Talish's *Fathiyya-i-Ibriyya* on Ahom matchlocks.
- Lachit's maidam (burial mound) at Hoolungapara, built by Udayaditya Singha in 1672.

### 4. Bukka Raya I: REWRITE

*Vijayanagara, r. 1356-1377. 4,978 words. Narrator: Ibrahim al-Malabari, a fictional Malabar Muslim mercenary dictating his memoirs around 1376.*

**Where it stands**
- It covers 1327 to about 1356: the fall of Kampili, captivity in Delhi, the founding of the city, and the narrator's slow exclusion.
- The chapter ends before its king's reign begins.
- It contradicts itself: Harihara dies in 1356, after which Bukka summons the narrator "in 1354".
- The outsider narrator, a Muslim in a Hindu king's service, is the right idea.
- The fight in the Kampili lane is some of the best combat in the book.

**What's wrong**
- **The central argument inverts the record.** The chapter has Bukka dismiss his Muslim adviser to protect "the last great Hindu empire standing against Islamic expansion". The documents show the opposite:
  - In 1352 Bukka styled himself *hindurayasuratrana*, "Sultan among Hindu kings". His brother Marappa had used the title in 1347.
  - The court adopted Islamicate dress and titles (Wagoner, 1996).
  - His best-documented religious act is the 1368 Shravanabelagola accord, ordering the Srivaishnavas to protect the Jains.
- **The captivity-and-conversion story is contested, and the chapter takes a side without saying so.**
  - It rests on Ibn Battuta's report that the sons of Kampila were converted and made amirs; whether they were the Sangama brothers is disputed.
  - It also rests on late Kannada and Sanskrit texts.
  - The court's own inscriptions never mention a conversion.
- **Vidyaranya:** his presence at the founding is later tradition, shown here as eyewitness fact. The founding myth took shape about two centuries later (Kulke, 1985). He is also given invented communal lines.
- **The brothers:** in the traditions they are treasury officers or commanders, not "Hindu princes".
- **Anachronisms:**
  - "Moplah", ten times. This is the British colonial spelling, and in the 14th century there were Christian and Jewish Mappilas too.
  - "Zamorin" and "Calicut" are European forms; Ibn Battuta wrote *al-Samiri*.
  - *thekkini* is used for the monsoon, but it is the south wing of a Kerala house.
  - Concentric walls are called "Islamic military architecture", but Warangal already had them.
  - "liaison officer".
- **Notes:**
  - "Some territorial claims have been moderated" is leftover boilerplate.
  - The Author's Note says "Warangal-to-Delhi"; the chapter uses Kampili.

**What works**
- "A Delhi footman swung a flanged iron mace that caught my sword arm just above the wrist. Bone cracked"
- The river purification scene: "the cheer that went up sounded like a kingdom exhaling".
- The ending: "Two dozen pearls" and "I tell them no."

**The restart**
- **Timeline:** set it inside Bukka's reign.
  - The spine is the Bahmani war of 1362-67.
  - The counter-movement is his son Kumara Kampana's Madurai campaign (1371).
- **Religious politics:** match the evidence: a court that borrows a sultan's title and dress while courting Brahmin legitimacy.
- **Narrator:**
  - Ibrahim falls to court faction, not a purity purge.
  - He trades in something that fits the period: warhorses shipped through the west-coast ports.
  - He hears the captivity story as a rumour, and doubts it.

**Material to use**
- The 1352 *suratrana* title, and Wagoner on the Islamicate tunic and cap (*kabayi* and *kullayi*) worn at court.
- The 1368 Shravanabelagola inscription.
- The 1346 Sringeri festival of the five Sangama brothers.
- Ferishta on the war of 1362-67:
  - the quarrel over coinage;
  - Mudgal;
  - the siege of Vijayanagara;
  - the pact to spare non-combatants.

  Treat his claim that artillery was used as doubtful.
- Gangadevi's *Madhura Vijayam*, her poem on her husband's Madurai campaign.
- The return of Ranganatha's image to Srirangam (1371).
- Ibn Battuta on the fall of Kampili.
- **Verify before using:** the 1374 embassy to Ming China, widely repeated but unconfirmed.

### 5. Jatavarman Sundara Pandyan I: SUBSTANTIAL REVISION

*Pandya, Madurai, 1251-1268. 3,669 words. Narrator: "Thandal Raman Pillai", a fictional fleet commander.*

**Where it stands**
- The skeleton is right: the Lanka campaign of 1258, the death of the Hoysala king Someshvara, and the Srirangam gold.
- The Kannanur breach and the tulabhara, the king weighed against gold, are among the better scenes in the book.
- But the opening is false, the middle third is essay, and there are two endings.

**What's wrong**
- **The opening kills a real man who survived.**
  - The Sri Lankan chronicle, the *Culavamsa*, has Prince Virabahu defeating the invader Chandrabhanu of Tambralinga in 1247 and again around 1262.
  - The Pandyas' quarry in Lanka was Chandrabhanu. He submitted in 1258, and Jatavarman Vira Pandya killed him around 1263.
- **The "Pandya lake" has no evidence.** The KDP line claims "Tamil fleets turned the Indian Ocean into a Pandya lake".
  - The only naval activity recorded in this reign is armies ferried across the Palk Strait.
  - The Pandyas grew rich from the sea without ruling it: pearls, and the horse trade Marco Polo and Wassaf describe.
- **Events from the next reign:**
  - The Tooth Relic was taken around 1284, under Maravarman Kulasekara I.
  - So was the 1279 defeat of the last Chola king.
  - `research_findings_comprehensive.md` files both under this chapter.
- **The northern war is garbled.**
  - The enemy was the Telugu Chodas of Nellore; the Krishna-Godavari valley the chapter names was Kakatiya country.
  - The real campaign is missing: Kanchi taken, the Kakatiya Ganapati beaten at Mudugur, and a victor's anointing (*virabhisheka*) at Nellore.
- **Details:**
  - The Hoysala emblem is Sala fighting a tiger; the boar is Chalukyan.
  - *Pon Veintha Perumal* means "the lord who roofed [the shrine] with gold", not "covered the world".
  - The dates disagree: c. 1251-1270 in the subtitle, c. 1251-1268 in the note. Use 1251-1268.
- **The notes misstate what is attested.**
  - The Editor's Note grounds the tribute terms in inscriptions, but only the jewels and elephants are attested.
  - The hostages, the ten thousand *kahavanus*, the storm and the 43-day siege are invented.
  - The Balban alliance in the Author's Note was cut from the chapter in December 2025.
- **Anachronisms:**
  - "Captain" and "Thandal" don't fit a Pandya commander. A *tindal* was a lascar petty officer, a term attested in English from 1698.
  - *Kattaris* at a 1262 siege are too early.

**What works**
- "the sweet rot of corpses too numerous to remove, and beneath it all the sharp reek of lime"
- The eight-year-old hostage: "He did not cry, but his eyes held the blankness of shock."
- "close enough that I could smell the betel on his breath"

**The restart**
- **Lanka:** rebuild it around the real three-way struggle: a Sinhalese appeal, Chandrabhanu's fleet, and his death.
- **Gold as the through-line:** first the plunder, then the scale at Srirangam, then the golden roof.
- **Narrator:** a field commander, or a Srirangam temple accountant who weighs the plunder.
- **Structure:** replace the essay sections with one scene, the anointing at Nellore, and keep one ending.
- **Blurb:** "carried Pandya arms from Lanka to Nellore, killed a Hoysala king, and weighed himself in gold at Srirangam."

**Material to use**
- Chandrabhanu's son Savakanmaindan, installed at Jaffna by the Pandyas.
- The Pandya flag raised at Konamalai (Trincomalee).
- The Hoysala general Singana, killed in the war.
- The *Koyil Olugu*, the Srirangam temple chronicle, on his gifts and repeated tulabharas.
- The Pandya system of co-ruling brothers, Marco Polo's "five brother kings". Its collapse in 1310 let Malik Kafur in, a hand-off to chapter 2.

### 6. Simhana II: REWRITE

*Seuna (Yadava), Devagiri, c. 1210-1246. 3,407 words. Narrator: Vaidyanatha Shastri, a fictional Sanskrit pandit.*

**Where it stands**
- It opens with a drought in 1204, before Simhana's reign, and a grain decree the villagers can't read.
- Six summary sections follow.
- It ends on a secret manual for a revolution to "liberate India".
- The Deccan's greatest war-king of his century gets no campaign, siege or court crisis.

**What's wrong**
- **The inscriptions contradict the thesis.**
  - Yadava inscriptions are mostly Kannada and Sanskrit.
  - Marathi appears in about two hundred of them, usually as a translation of or addition to the Kannada or Sanskrit text.
  - Marathi becomes the dominant language only in the dynasty's last half-century, after Simhana.
  - His own court worked in Sanskrit. His accountant Sarngadeva wrote the *Sangita Ratnakara*, and his astrologer Changadeva's college at Patan (1207) is recorded in a Sanskrit inscription.
  - The Marathi turn belongs to his successors: the minister Hemadri, the Mahanubhava sect (founded 1267; *Lilacharitra* 1278), and the *Dnyaneshwari* (1290).
- **Kannada is never mentioned.** It was the Yadavas' most common inscriptional language. So "For a thousand years, Sanskrit has been the language of kings" is false for the Deccan, and Marathi inscriptions go back to Akshi (1012).
- **The literary dates don't fit.**
  - The note dates Mukundaraja's *Vivekasindhu* to "c. 1188". That date is contested, and in any case it precedes the reign.
  - Chakradhar's order was founded 21 years after Simhana died.
- **No Kakatiya war:** the Kakatiya king Ganapati, once a captive at Devagiri, was a vassal.
- **Dates:** use c. 1210-1246, and note that the accession is disputed.
- **Anachronisms:**
  - *kamavisdar*, a Peshwa-era office;
  - *gaddar* and *hazara*, both Persian;
  - *dhanyawad* as "thank you", a modern usage;
  - "tsunami";
  - "liberate India".
- **Undisclosed invention:** only the secret manual is disclosed. These are also invented:
  - the famine council;
  - the Marathi-first schools;
  - the inscription policy;
  - the narrator's beating.

**What works**
- "The earth cracked like old leather, fissures wide enough to swallow a man's foot."
- The market verses in which a pompous Sanskrit scholar is out-argued by a village woman.

**The restart**
- **Framing:** Simhana as war-king. He:
  - crushed the Shilaharas of Kolhapur;
  - pushed back the Hoysalas;
  - raided Malwa and Gujarat;
  - made Devagiri the prize Alauddin Khilji rode south to take fifty years later.
- **Language as texture, not thesis:** a kingdom that ruled in three tongues, Sanskrit for the gods, Kannada for the chanceries, Marathi for the fields.
- **Narrator:** a clerk in Sarngadeva's accounts office.
- **Spine:** the Gujarat war.
- **Ending:** foreshadow Hemadri, Chakradhar, and Alauddin's raid on Devagiri in 1296.

**Material to use**
- Jayasimha Suri's play *Hammira-mada-mardana*: Gujarat beset by Iltutmish, Simhana and the Paramaras, and the minister Vastupala's spies turning the Lata chief against Simhana.
- The *Lekhapaddhati* treaty of "Maharajadhiraja Simhanadeva" with Lavanaprasada (c. 1231).
- The Brahmin general Kholeshvara and his Ambe inscription (1228).
- Changadeva's Patan inscription (1207).
- The fall of Panhala (c. 1209-12).

### 7. Balban: REWRITE

*Delhi Sultanate, r. 1266-1287. 3,818 words. Narrator: "Imad al-Din Raihan", writing in chains as he dies of fever while Balban is dying.*

**Where it stands**
- It is a memoir from a cell, which makes it the same chapter as Alauddin's: a condemned insider, the same chain sores, bones breaking like "green wood", and the same spy-state moral.
- There is no battle.
- The last three sections, 28 percent of the chapter, repeat one moral.

**What's wrong**
- **The narrator is a real man, misused.**
  - Imad-ud-din Raihan was an Indian-born Muslim, reportedly a eunuch, who led the court faction that displaced Balban in 1253.
  - He was dismissed in 1254, joined a revolt, and was killed around 1255-56 (Minhaj-i-Siraj).
  - He was never Balban's "slave companion" or adviser, and he was long dead by 1286.
  - The Editor's Note and the Author's Note both call him fictional.
  - The chapter also gives him the work of Imad-ul-Mulk, Balban's real head of the army department.
- **Alauddin's reforms are credited to Balban.** Horse branding (*dagh*), descriptive rolls of soldiers (*chehra*) and cash salaries were Alauddin's (Barani). The error recurs in the Author's Note and the research notes.
- **Balban's character is inverted.**
  - The chapter has him preach "promotions based on merit, not lineage".
  - Barani's Balban did the opposite: he refused office to a man whose father had been a slave, and would not receive a merchant chief.
- **The legacy is overclaimed.**
  - His settlement collapsed within four years, ending in the Khalji coup of 1290.
  - The *barid*, the royal news-writer, was an old caliphal institution that he enforced, not one he invented.
- **Dates:** 1266-1286 should be 1266-1287.
- **Undisclosed invention:**
  - the skull tower in Mewat;
  - the strangled administrator;
  - Vishnu Das, disclosed only in the Author's Note.

  Meanwhile the Editor's Note calls "the administrative innovations attributed to Balban" accurate.
- **Anachronisms:**
  - "the invisible hand" (Adam Smith);
  - "Blood and Iron" (Bismarck; a modern historians' label for Balban's policy);
  - "genocide", "suicide pact", "feudal levies";
  - a narrator who prophesies the Tughlaqs and the Mughals.

  "System" and its derivatives appear 28 times.

**What works**
- "I counted the first fifty. Then I stopped counting."
- "'Watch,' Balban commanded... 'This is what order costs.'"
- "At the end, he called out for his mother."
- The Baqbaq, Haibat Khan and Tughril material.

**The restart**
- **The witness:** choose an honest one, and drop the prison. Two options:
  - a *barid* posted to Badaun after his predecessor was hanged over the city gate for failing to report a crime;
  - Amir Khusrau, who served Balban's son and was captured by the Mongols in 1285.
- **The documented Balban:**
  - the prostration and foot-kissing rituals;
  - the king who never laughed in public and claimed descent from the Persian hero Afrasiyab;
  - the humbling of the Forty;
  - justice by public example;
  - terror in Mewat and Katehar;
  - contempt for the low-born.
- **A battle and a heart:**
  - Tughril's revolt and the gibbets of Lakhnauti;
  - the death of Prince Muhammad fighting the Mongols in 1285, and the old Sultan's collapse;
  - the nobles setting aside his heir for Kaiqubad: the road to the Khalji coup and chapter 2.
- **Framing:** "The Shadow of God: the slave who made Delhi kneel."
- **If both Delhi chapters stay:** give them different ground, and put them side by side, Balban first.
  - **Alauddin:** the Mongol war, the price of bread, and the converts.
  - **Balban:** the theatre of divine kingship, with the Mongols only as the frontier that kills his son.

**Material to use**
- Minhaj-i-Siraj's *Tabaqat-i-Nasiri* on the crisis of 1253-54. He is a partisan witness: he lost his post under Raihan.
- Barani on:
  - Malik Baqbaq flogged to death, and the Badaun news-writer hanged for failing to report it;
  - Haibat Khan handed over to his victim's widow;
  - Delhi's western gates shut at afternoon prayer for fear of the Mewatis.
- The Lakhnauti gibbets, and Balban's warning to his son Bughra Khan.
- Khusrau's elegies for Prince Muhammad.
- Balban's tomb at Mehrauli, often cited as the first true arch in India.

### 8. Kapilendra Deva: REWRITE

*Gajapati Odisha, r. 1434/35-1466/67. 4,527 words. Narrator: Malik Hussain al-Deccani, a fictional Bahmani cavalry commander.*

**Where it stands**
- War elephants emerging from the mist at Devarakonda is a real opening image. By the fourth paragraph, though, the chapter is an essay.
- The narrator sounds like a modern defence analyst: "innovation" appears twelve times, and "coordinat-" seventeen.
- It ends on an invented, intercepted plan to "eliminate Islamic power" in South India.

**What's wrong**
- **"The largest Hindu realm since the Mauryas" is false.**
  - The Guptas, Pratiharas and Rashtrakutas ruled more.
  - Rajendra I's Cholas and Devaraya II's Vijayanagara were comparable.
  - The Mauryas were not a Hindu dynasty.
  - Defensible instead: R. C. Majumdar's "most powerful Hindu king of his time", whose realm briefly reached from the lower Ganga to the Kaveri (1464).
- **Devarakonda is misdated and misdescribed.**
  - The text says 1451; the battle was in 1458.
  - The Bahmanis were besieging a Velama chief, who called Kapilendra in. Hamvira relieved the fort, and Kapilendra added "lord of Gulbarga" to his titles.
  - The chapter erases the Hindu ally the battle was fought for.
  - It also omits the reversal of 1461, when a Gajapati thrust toward Bidar was defeated.
- **The Vijayanagara war runs backwards.**
  - Devaraya II's general Mallappa Odeya drove Kapilendra back at Rajahmundry in 1444 (Draksharama inscription).
  - The Gajapati advance came only after Devaraya died in 1446: Rajahmundry 1448, Kondavidu 1454, Udayagiri 1460, Srirangam 1464.
  - The "coordinated" offensive of 1444 is invented.
- **The *rauta* "masterstroke" was inherited.**
  - Ruling as the deputy of Jagannatha was the ideology of the Ganga king Anangabhima III, two centuries earlier.
  - No assassination is recorded. Kapilendra took the throne while Bhanudeva IV was away on campaign.
- **Wrong sultan:** a report written after Kapilendra's death goes to "Sultan Ahmad Shah", who died in 1458.
- **Dates:** the subtitle ends in 1467, the note and text in 1466. Write r. 1434/35-1466/67.
- **The notes claim sources that don't exist.**
  - The Editor's Note cites "authenticated Islamic military accounts" by Bahmani commanders. There are none.
  - The Persian record is court chronicles written 130 to 150 years later (the *Burhan-i Ma'asir* and Ferishta), plus Mahmud Gawan's letters.
  - Undisclosed inventions: the 5,000 elephants, the treasurer and spymaster, the Kondavidu siege methods, and the royal audience.
- **The framing is anachronistic.** The note speaks of "real patterns of... Hindu resistance", but alliances crossed faith lines constantly:
  - Kapilendra's longest wars were against Hindu Vijayanagara and the Reddis.
  - The Velama chiefs switched sides.
  - After his death, his son Hamvira brought Bahmani troops against his own brother.
- **Anachronisms:**
  - "European trading companies"; the Portuguese reached India only in 1498.
  - Ottoman-style artillery.
  - "Captain" and "Sergeant".
  - Ashoka as a remembered benchmark, though his script was not deciphered until 1837.
  - *asis* and *tomaras*, dictionary words. The paika infantry fought with the *khanda* sword and the *phari* shield.

**What works**
- "My horse, a veteran of twelve campaigns, reared in terror fifty yards from the elephant line"
- "The seal was ours. The signatures were ours. Only the men who carried out the attack had not been"
- Sarala Das's Odia *Mahabharata*, sung in the camps.

**The restart**
- **Set-piece:** rebuild it on the real Devarakonda (1458), with the failed thrust toward Bidar (1461) as the Bahmani counter-punch.
- **Ending:** the real twist. Hamvira rides with Bahmani troops to take his brother's throne, so the Muslim narrator helps crown a Hindu prince.
- **Structure:** build the chapter on the title "Gajapati Gaudeshwara Navakoti Karnata Kalabargeshwara", one clause for each enemy.
- **Framing:** "The Usurper Who Carried Odisha from the Ganga to the Kaveri."

**Material to use**
- The Gopinathpur inscription (c. 1447): the minister Gopinatha Mahapatra drives back the Bengal sultan.
- The Draksharama inscription (1444).
- Hamvira's Srirangam inscription (1464).
- Kapilendra's edict at the Lingaraja temple, warning disloyal vassals.
- Ferishta and the *Burhan-i Ma'asir* on 1458, 1461 and Hamvira's Bahmani alliance.
- The *Madala Panji*, the Puri temple chronicle, on the succession.

### 9. Vidyadhara: REWRITE

*Chandela, c. 1003-1035. 2,616 words. Narrator: "Captain" Siyavash ibn Ahmad, a fictional Persian cavalry officer serving Mahmud of Ghazni.*

**Where it stands**
- Vidyadhara is almost unknown to English readers, which makes him an excellent pick.
- But the chapter's premise runs against its sources, and its climax is invented.

**What's wrong**
- **The title and blurb reverse the record.** The title says "The King Who Made Mahmud of Ghazni Retreat"; the KDP line says "night attacks forced Mahmud of Ghazni to retreat".
  - **1019:** the Chandela army withdrew at night without a battle (only Ibn al-Athir records a day's fighting), and Mahmud took 580 elephants from its camp.
  - **1022:** Gwalior bought off a four-day siege with 35 elephants. Kalinjar ended with the king sending out 300 elephants, begging for safety, and adding verses in Mahmud's praise. Nizamuddin and Firishta say Mahmud then granted him fifteen forts.
  - No Chandela night attack is recorded. Mahmud's real Indian setbacks came at Lohkot in Kashmir, in 1015 and 1021.
- **The climax is invented:** an elephant sortie on the fifteenth night of the siege, placed after the truce.
- **Dates and facts:**
  - "c. 1018-1029" should be c. 1003-1035.
  - The Kandariya Mahadeva temple is complete (c. 1025-1050), not "never finished".
  - Rajyapala of Kannauj was not executed. He was killed "in a great battle" by the Kachchhapaghata ruler Arjuna, at Vidyadhara's urging (Dubkund inscription, 1088).
- **Anachronisms:**
  - "Rajput", four times in the text and again in the note;
  - the katar;
  - "Bundelkhand"; the period name was Jejakabhukti;
  - "Captain";
  - a Persian cavalry commander in an army built on Turkish *ghulams* (slave-soldiers).
- **Notes:**
  - The Historical Note invents a "later tradition" crediting his fortification and elephant warfare.
  - It should say that the 1019 opponent's identity is contested. The chronicles' "Nanda" may be his father Ganda, though most scholars now name Vidyadhara.

**What works**
- "The chronicles will say we won a tactical victory, that the Hindus retreated in the night. But I was there." The unreliable-witness device is worth keeping.
- "elephant musk thick enough to taste"
- The death of the narrator's brother Bahram.

**The restart.** The attested story is better than the invented one. Vidyadhara:
- had a rival king killed for running from the Turks;
- then twice declined to fight Mahmud himself;
- kept his fortress, army and throne with a bargain of elephants and verse;
- and poured his wealth into Khajuraho's greatest temple.

A candidate narrator is a Hindu interpreter in Mahmud's camp who has to translate the king's verses for the Sultan.

**Material to use**
- Nizamuddin's 1019 standoff: the armies camped on opposite riverbanks, an envoy's demand, and Mahmud dismayed by the army he surveys from high ground.
- The 300 elephants driven out of Kalinjar without riders.
- Al-Biruni: "Jajahuti... The capital of the country is Kajuraha."
- The Mahoba inscription, casting Bhoja and the Kalachuri king as Vidyadhara's "pupils". It is the hand-off to chapter 10.
- The "Virimda" inscription on the Kandariya Mahadeva, as a closing image.

### 10. Bhoja: REWRITE

*Paramara, Malwa, c. 1010-1055. 2,583 words. Narrator: "Abu Nasr al-Utbi", arriving at Dhara three days after it fell in 1055.*

**Where it stands**
- The first half is scene; the last three sections are essay.
- It ends on a secret network of Bhoja's followers "from Cordoba to Samarkand".
- A king sold on "books and battles" gets no battle.

**What's wrong**
- **The narrator is real.**
  - Al-Utbi (c. 961 to 1036-1040) was Mahmud of Ghazni's secretary and the author of the *Kitab al-Yamini*.
  - He had been dead fifteen to twenty years by 1055.
  - Both the Editor's Note and the Author's Note call him fictional.
- **The centrepiece has no source.**
  - Nothing records a library massacre or the burning of forty thousand manuscripts.
  - Tradition points the other way: the Gujarat king Siddharaja Jayasimha carried Bhoja's library from Dhara to Patan around 1135.
  - What Merutunga records being taken in 1055 is Bhoja's golden pavilion.
- **The Bhojpur dam:** the breach is traditionally Hoshang Shah's, in the 15th century.
- **Dates:**
  - The reign should start c. 1010, not 1000; his earliest record is the Modasa plates of 1011.
  - The succession is missing, and so is Paramara Malwa's survival until 1305.
- **The notes call history fiction.**
  - The Historical Note calls the three-king alliance invented. It is attested: Jayasimha II's Roogi (1019) and Kulenur (1028) inscriptions describe Bhoja, the Kalachuri Gangeya and Rajendra Chola attacking together.
  - The same note describes letters that were cut from the text in December 2025.
  - The Author's Note still lists those letters, a "metallurgy" manuscript, and a defeat "by the Chalukyas". His final enemies were the Kalachuri Lakshmi-Karna and the Chaulukya Bhima I.
- **The lament is legend.** "Adya Dhara niradhara" comes from Ballala's much later *Bhoja-prabandha*, where Kalidasa recites it. It is not a record of Bhoja's death.
- **Anachronisms:**
  - the talwar;
  - "medieval" in the narrator's mouth;
  - "the pen is mightier than the sword" (Bulwer-Lytton, 1839);
  - leather-bound books, where the period book was the palm-leaf *pothi*;
  - the Gangu Teli proverb quoted as current in the 11th century.
- **Handle with care: Bhojshala.**
  - The name was coined in 1903.
  - The site's religious character is in active litigation. The Madhya Pradesh High Court declared it a temple on 15 May 2026, and the Muslim side has appealed to the Supreme Court.
  - The chapter should not present one side's claim as settled history.

**What works**
- "Now they warm the hands of soldiers who cannot read."
- "The respect of his enemies lasted only as long as the funeral pyre burned."
- The lament, if framed as legend.

**The restart**
- **Framing:** the scholar-king who lost his wars and won his name. The arc runs:
  1. the humiliation and execution of his uncle Munja by the Chalukya Tailapa II (c. 995);
  2. the three-front war against Jayasimha II;
  3. Someshvara I's sack of Dhara;
  4. his death from illness as two kings invaded at once in 1055;
  5. a legend that outlived every one of his enemies.
- **His flaws:** draw them from recorded legend rather than invention. In the Jain story, he burns the poet Dhanapala's *Tilakamanjari*.
- **Narrator:** Damara, Bhima I's envoy to Dhara in Merutunga's account, or an officer in the invading camp.

**Material to use**
- The Udaipur Prashasti on his death: "Dhara was filled with a dense darkness by his foes."
- Merutunga on the broken partition agreement and the golden pavilion.
- Bilhana on Someshvara's sack of Dhara.
- Chapter 31 of the *Samarangana Sutradhara*: mechanical birds and lamp-filling dolls, and the author's refusal to say how to build them "for the sake of secrecy".
- Siddharaja's carts of books.
- The unfinished Bhojeshwar temple at Bhojpur.
- **Verify before using:** al-Biruni's reported mention of "Bhojadeva" at Dhar. Check it in Sachau's translation.

## How to Restart

1. **Settle the four decisions** at the top: title, order, lineup and length.
2. **Hold every rewrite to five rules** that come straight out of this review:
   - **Witness:** the narrator is invented, or else a real person used truthfully. Never a real person relabelled as fiction.
   - **Headline:** the claim is one the sources support.
   - **Set-piece:** at least one, in scene: before, contact, turning point, resolution, after, with the narrator's position always clear.
   - **Ending:** close on an image, not a moral.
   - **Notes:** write them last, against the final text, and disclose every invention.
3. **Write in pairs, in reign order.** Each pair shares a world, so the hand-offs get built along the way:
   1. **Vidyadhara and Bhoja.** Start here:
      - they are the shortest now;
      - they are the richest in unused sources;
      - the Mahoba inscription links them;
      - in reign order they open the book.
   2. **Simhana and Jatavarman.**
   3. **Balban and Alauddin.**
   4. **Bukka and Kapilendra.**
   5. **Lachit and Marthanda Varma,** with the Colachel prologue.
4. **Take each chapter through the same cycle:**
   1. its brief from this review;
   2. a draft;
   3. a fact-check with the project's `indian-history-fact-checker` agent;
   4. an edit with the `book-editor` agent;
   5. the notes.
5. **Finish the rest of the book:**

   - Recompile `manuscript_v1.md`.

## Scholarship to Start From

**Across the whole period**
- Richard M. Eaton, *India in the Persianate Age, 1000-1765* (2019). It covers the whole span of the book, and it models the cross-faith reading the chapters need.
- Peter Jackson, *The Delhi Sultanate: A Political and Military History* (1999). This is the standard account of the Mongol invasions (chapters 2 and 7).
- Iqtidar Alam Khan, *Gunpowder and Firearms: Warfare in Medieval India* (2004). It gives the firearms chronology (chapters 3, 4 and 8).

**By chapter**
- **Chapter 1:** Mark de Lannoy, *The Kulasekhara Perumals of Travancore* (Leiden, 1997), built on the Dutch archives.
- **Chapter 3:**
  - S. K. Bhuyan's editions of the Assam Buranjis, and his *Lachit Barphukan and His Times*.
  - C. R. Boxer's article on the real Captain João Ribeiro, in the *Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society*.
- **Chapter 4:**
  - Phillip B. Wagoner, "'Sultan among Hindu Kings'", *Journal of Asian Studies* 55:4 (1996).
  - Hermann Kulke, "Maharajas, Mahants and Historians" (1985).
  - Burton Stein, *Vijayanagara* (1989).
- **Chapter 5:** K. A. Nilakanta Sastri, *The Pandyan Kingdom* (1929).
- **Chapter 6:**
  - Christian Lee Novetzke, *The Quotidian Revolution* (2016).
  - A. V. Narasimha Murthy, *The Sevunas of Devagiri* (1971).
- **Chapter 8:** Hermann Kulke's work on Anangabhima III and the Jagannatha kingship.
- **Chapter 9:** Sisir Kumar Mitra, *The Early Rulers of Khajuraho* (1958).
- **Chapter 10:** Pratipal Bhatia, *The Paramaras* (1970).
